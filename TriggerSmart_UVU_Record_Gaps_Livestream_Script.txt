UVU RECORD GAPS — TRIGGERSMART LIVESTREAM

INTRO

One of the most important security messages in the Charlie Kirk case appears twice inside Utah Valley University’s own independent After-Action Report.

And the two versions do not match.

In the report’s verbatim text section, the exchange is dated September 8 at 3:12 p.m. Dan Flood warns Chief Jeff Long that there is student roof access close to where Charlie Kirk will be set up. Flood asks for controlled access or permission to place one of his own people there. Long responds:

“I got you covered.”

But in the report’s own timeline, what appears to be that same exchange is placed at 4:11 p.m. and the response becomes:

“We got you covered.”

[SHOW #1]

Now stop there.

I am not telling you one changed pronoun proves somebody altered evidence. I am not telling you a one-hour discrepancy proves a cover-up. Reports contain mistakes. Timelines get compiled from different source files. Human beings type the wrong time.

But this is not a random typo in a parking memo.

This four-word assurance has become part of the legal and public argument over whether UVU understood the elevated-position problem before Charlie Kirk was killed.

So tonight the rule is simple.

When the document gives us an answer, we use the document.

When the document gives us two answers, we do not pick the one we like. We identify the conflict and ask for the original record.

And once you start doing that, the September 8 text is not the only loose end.

The morning Charlie was killed, UVU’s report says police received an anonymous tip about a potential EMP device that a suspect might use at the event.

The day before Charlie was killed, a released UVU email says someone wanted the university to “get a message to Charlie Kirk.”

Later that same day, two men showed up at a university office asking to talk to police “about a plan.” Staff first thought senators might be involved. Then somebody corrected the record and said the men were not from a senator’s office.

Months later, prosecutors returned to Mike Mitchell, the family friend involved in Tyler Robinson’s surrender, and collected a new handwritten statement. That statement became State’s Exhibit 14. The judge admitted it. The public did not get to see it. When prosecutors later asked to publish just three or four lines, the judge denied that too.

None of those facts proves the others are connected.

That is exactly why this story is worth telling carefully.

Because what we have is not one magic document.

We have a trail of records, gaps, corrections, redactions and unanswered questions.

And if we line them up in the right order, the story becomes much clearer.

# 1. FIRST, WHAT UVU ITSELF ADMITS

Before we look at anything unusual, we need to establish the baseline using the university’s own review.

UVU commissioned an external review to examine planning, preparedness, response and recovery surrounding September 10, 2025. The university says the review drew on more than seventy interviews, thousands of pages of records, communications, dispatch information, digital material and other evidence.

[SHOW #2]

The review does not say every UVU employee acted recklessly or maliciously. In fact, the project team states that it did not find UVU employees or agents acting in bad faith or in willful and wanton disregard for safety.

That matters.

If we are going to use the review when it documents failures, then we also have to use it when it gives an exculpatory conclusion.

But the report still identifies serious weaknesses.

The event lacked a comprehensive written Security Assessment or Event Action Plan.

Planning did not formalize assessments of elevated vantage points and building access.

Coordination between campus police and private security was limited.

There was no completed joint pre-event security briefing and walk-through.

And there was no dedicated assignment for real-time security video monitoring during the event.

Think about what that means.

The system was not empty. There were police. There was private security. There were cameras. There were meetings. There were texts. There was a command post.

But the review’s criticism is that those pieces were not pulled together into one sufficiently structured security process.

That becomes crucial when we reach the text exchange.

Because if you do not have a comprehensive written plan, a four-word text can become one of the best records we have of what two people believed had been agreed to.

So now let’s read the actual words.

# 2. THE FOUR WORDS THAT NOW CARRY A YEAR OF ARGUMENT

On September 8, two days before the event, Dan Flood sends Chief Long a message.

The AAR’s verbatim section says Flood forwards a warning from the student group stating there is student roof access pretty close to where Charlie Kirk will be set up. It specifically references the Sorensen Student Center and staircases going up toward elevated walkways.

Chief Long asks:

“You want access to the roof?”

Flood answers that he has been told students have access above them. He says it would be nice either to have controlled access or allow one of his own people to be there.

Then Long responds:

“I got you covered.”

[SHOW #3]

That is the version printed in the report’s verbatim exchange.

Now compare that with the report’s timeline.

There the exchange is placed at 4:11 p.m., not 3:12, and the answer is summarized as:

“We got you covered.”

[SHOW #4]

There are two separate differences.

The time.

And the pronoun.

Again, neither one by itself establishes misconduct.

But there is a reason this specific discrepancy deserves attention.

The family’s September 2026 notice of claim later described the security warning much more broadly. Reuters reported that the notice alleges Turning Point USA warned university police about security risks from rooftops overlooking the speaking area and that Chief Long answered with the assurance that they were covered.

[SHOW #5]

The AAR interprets the exchange more narrowly.

According to the report, the main location being discussed was the Hall of Flags outdoor pedestrian walkway directly above the canopy where Charlie would speak.

That is important because the official criminal case places the alleged shooter on the Losee Center roof.

Those are not the same place.

UVU police did act on the Hall of Flags concern. On the morning of September 10, the report says officers cleared roughly eighty to one hundred people from the portion of the walkway directly above the canopy, taped the area off and posted officers there.

So if the question is, “Did Chief Long completely ignore the area Flood was worried about?” the report gives us a pretty strong answer.

No.

Police did clear and restrict that area.

But that creates a different question.

What did “covered” mean to the two men when the message was sent?

Flood’s forwarded warning used the language of roof access near Charlie’s setup. Long himself asked whether Flood wanted roof access. The report later says the exchange reveals their primary focus was the Hall of Flags walkway.

That interpretation may be correct.

But the best way to know what the exchange meant is not to reverse-engineer it from a report written a year later.

It is to examine the original message record and the surrounding communications.

And the report itself identifies the thing that should have removed most of this ambiguity.

A face-to-face security walk-through.

The AAR says Long proposed meeting Flood on the walkway at 11 a.m. the morning of the event.

That meeting did not occur.

The review calls the absence of a formal briefing and coordinated walk-through involving Brian Harpole, Dan Flood and the TPUSA security team a “critical missed opportunity.”

[SHOW #6]

And that phrase is much more important to me than trying to turn “I” versus “we” into something sinister.

Because imagine the meeting had happened.

Stand in the courtyard.

Point at the Hall of Flags.

That area is ours.

Point at the Losee Center.

Who has that?

Point at the surrounding buildings.

Who is watching windows, stairwells, balconies and rooftops?

Who is monitoring cameras?

Who calls Orem if the crowd is bigger than expected?

A ten-minute conversation with people physically pointing at the same structures could have eliminated the disagreement we are now trying to reconstruct from text messages.

This is where the story starts to change.

The real issue may not be that one security concern was ignored.

It may be that everybody was solving individual problems without a sufficiently complete shared picture of the venue.

And then, before most of the security team even arrives on September 10, another piece of information comes in.

# 3. 7:23 A.M. — THE TIP THAT SOUNDS LIKE INTERNET FICTION UNTIL YOU READ THE REPORT

The incident-day timeline says that at 7:23 a.m., Chief Long contacted the Utah Statewide Information and Analysis Center regarding an anonymous threat.

The report’s narrative gives the time as approximately 7:30 a.m.

The subject of the threat was a potential electronic-magnetic-pulse device — an EMP device — that a suspect might use at the event.

[SHOW #7]

That sentence is extraordinary enough that it needs to be handled very carefully.

The report does not say an EMP device was found.

It does not say an EMP device was used.

It does not say the tipster had reliable knowledge.

It says there was an anonymous tip warning about a potential EMP device, Chief Long contacted SIAC, and SIAC responded that the threat was not credible.

Why did SIAC consider it not credible?

According to the AAR, the tipster appeared to have provided similar information in the past that had been determined unreliable.

Now notice the strange tension in those two sentences.

The tip is described as anonymous.

But SIAC apparently believes the tipster has a history.

That is not impossible at all.

Anonymous to the university does not necessarily mean technically unidentifiable to the system receiving the report. There could be an account, email address, phone number, reporting identifier, writing pattern or another method that allowed analysts to associate submissions.

The report simply does not tell us.

And that is the point where people online tend to make one of two mistakes.

The first mistake is to say:

“This proves there was an EMP attack.”

No, it absolutely does not.

The second mistake is to say:

“SIAC called it non-credible, so there is nothing left to examine.”

That also goes too far.

The narrow documentary question is much simpler.

What did the original tip actually say, and what exactly did SIAC send back?

SIAC is Utah’s state intelligence fusion center. Its own public description explains that it supports information sharing, threat assessment and public-safety intelligence among law-enforcement and government partners.

[SHOW #8]

So Chief Long appears to have done the reasonable thing when a strange threat arrived: he kicked it up to the people whose job includes evaluating threat information.

The missing piece is not whether he contacted them.

The report says he did.

The missing piece is the underlying communication.

Was the tip one vague sentence from somebody who had sent bizarre messages before?

Did it name a person?

Did it identify a location?

Did it mention a vehicle, backpack, stage equipment or a specific time?

We do not know.

And we should not pretend we know.

That is what makes the original email important.

The public debate around this exploded after the AAR came out, and Candace Owens discussed the EMP entry in her page-by-page review of the report.

[SHOW #9]

Her theories are commentary.

The tip itself is a documented fact.

Keep those categories separated.

That separation is what lets us ask a harder question without losing credibility.

The report describes SIAC rejecting the threat as non-credible, but it does not describe a follow-up step where the tip was passed to TPUSA’s security team when they arrived around nine.

Maybe it was.

Maybe it was not.

Maybe the threat was so obviously unreliable that there was no operational reason to brief them.

But the AAR does not give us the underlying record, and it does not describe a venue search specifically triggered by that tip.

So the fair conclusion is not “EMP.”

The fair conclusion is:

There was an EMP-related tip. It was evaluated and rejected. The original tip and response would tell us whether that judgment was obviously reasonable or whether more context matters.

And once you adopt that standard, the next record becomes even more interesting.

Because the day before the event, somebody wanted UVU to get a message to Charlie Kirk.

# 4. SEPTEMBER 9 — “HE WANTS US TO GET A MESSAGE TO CHARLIE KIRK”

This is UVU record 25-330:0080.

The email is dated September 9, 2025 at 2:23 p.m.

The subject is:

“FYI.”

The visible sentence says:

“He wants us to get a message to Charlie Kirk. His phone number is—”

Then the identifying information is redacted.

[SHOW #10]

That is all we can responsibly say from the visible record.

We do not know the message.

We do not know whether it was threatening.

We do not know whether it was friendly.

We do not know whether the person wanted to warn Charlie about something, ask him for something, meet him, complain about him or simply pass along a normal message.

And this is where hindsight can manipulate you.

Charlie Kirk was killed the next day.

So a sentence saying someone wanted to get a message to him feels ominous.

But that feeling is not evidence.

The right question is not:

“What scary thing can I imagine inside the redaction?”

The right question is:

“What happened to the message?”

Was it delivered?

Was the request rejected?

Was it forwarded to TPUSA?

Did anybody call the number?

Was it completely routine?

Those answers may exist somewhere outside the pages we have.

But the public release gives us the beginning of the branch and not the ending.

And that is what I mean by documentary negative space.

A redaction is not evidence of wrongdoing.

An omission is not evidence of wrongdoing.

But when you are reconstructing the final twenty-four hours before a high-profile assassination, an unresolved contact involving the victim is a legitimate record to follow.

Especially when the university’s own independent report does not appear to discuss it.

The AAR cannot include every message generated across a university. That would be absurd.

But the review says it examined thousands of pages of communications and digital evidence.

So this gives us a clean question for the record.

Was UVU 25-330:0080 reviewed and judged irrelevant, or did it sit outside what the project team received?

Either answer is useful.

And a few hours later, another unusual contact appears.

This one actually gets walked over to police.

# 5. TWO MEN, A “PLAN,” AND A STAFF THREAD TRYING TO FIGURE OUT WHO THEY WERE

The next records are UVU 25-330:0023 through 0025.

The conversation begins with a staff member saying Event Services had come to the office reporting that two senators were coming the next day.

[SHOW #11]

Then the thought continues onto the next page.

The staff member says two people had shown up at offices asking to talk to police “about a plan.”

The message says they got them to police.

[SHOW #12]

Now stop.

“About a plan” is one of those phrases that becomes dangerous when people start writing the rest of the sentence themselves.

A plan for what?

The event?

A senator visit?

Security?

Transportation?

Political coordination?

Something completely unrelated?

The record does not tell us.

So we are not going to tell the audience that “a plan” means a plot.

It does not.

What the record does tell us is that two people arrived, asked to speak with police about something described as a plan, and were taken to police.

Then the staff discussion starts trying to figure out who they were connected to.

There is discussion of senators.

And then comes the correction.

A later message says the two men were NOT from a senator’s office and describes them as GOP representatives who “played like they knew more than they did.”

[SHOW #13]

That is a strange little sequence.

But strange is not the same thing as criminal.

The useful question is whether the police contact produced any record.

If somebody walks into a university office the day before a nationally known speaker arrives, asks to speak with campus police about a plan, and staff physically get them to police, there may be a visitor record, officer note, email, incident log or follow-up communication.

Maybe there is none because the conversation was harmless and informal.

That would be an answer.

The problem right now is that we have the staff-side breadcrumb but not the police-side resolution.

The Americans for Transparency reconstruction puts these September 9 contacts together as two separate approaches that do not appear in the AAR narrative.

[SHOW #14]

And there is another technical detail worth noticing.

The Teams thread is stamped around 10:35 p.m. in the release. The reconstruction argues those Teams timestamps appear to be UTC, six hours ahead of Utah at the time, which would put the local conversation around 4:35 p.m.

That time conversion is their analytical reading, not a separate local timestamp printed by UVU.

This is exactly the type of thing that matters when people build internet timelines.

One person sees 10:35 and assumes Utah time.

Another person notices the software export is UTC and moves it six hours.

Suddenly the same event occupies two completely different places on the timeline.

Sound familiar?

That is why I keep coming back to source integrity.

The AAR gives us 3:12 and 4:11 for the Flood text.

The Teams export appears to require timezone interpretation.

The EMP entry is 7:23 in the timeline and approximately 7:30 in the narrative.

None of that automatically indicates manipulation.

It indicates that people doing timeline work need the original metadata whenever possible.

Now put the September 9 items beside each other, but do not connect them.

At 2:23, somebody wants a message delivered to Charlie Kirk.

Later that afternoon, assuming the UTC conversion is correct, two men appear asking to talk to police about a plan.

There is no public evidence we have found showing that the person behind the message is connected to those two men.

So I am not connecting them.

That is the difference between investigation and storytelling that outruns the evidence.

Investigation says:

“These are two unresolved contacts on the same day. Can we find a record connecting them?”

Conspiracy storytelling says:

“They happened on the same day, therefore they are connected.”

We are staying on the first side of that line.

And that brings us back to the larger failure the AAR itself describes.

Because individual unexplained contacts become much more important when the overall security system is fragmented.

# 6. THE BIGGER STORY IS NOT ONE TEXT — IT IS A SYSTEM BUILT OUT OF PIECES

This is where the After-Action Report is actually most useful.

It lets us stop arguing about whether one person “cared” enough or whether one text sounds suspicious and instead examine how the system was structured.

The report says UVU personnel made meaningful preparation efforts.

It also says the preparation process revealed important gaps.

The security assessment did not produce documented requirements for venue modification, controlled access, credentialing, screening or additional staffing.

UVU did not have reliable access to TPUSA ticketing data.

Coordination with TPUSA security was very limited.

There was no structured pre-event briefing or site walk-through involving Harpole, Flood and UVUPD.

Chief Long did not make a mutual-aid request to Orem police before the event, and additional outside resources were not requested after the larger crowd became apparent.

And there was no dedicated assignment for real-time security video monitoring.

[SHOW #15]

That is the structural story.

Not “nobody did anything.”

People did things.

Chief Long contacted SIAC.

UVU police cleared the Hall of Flags walkway.

Officers taped it off.

A command post was established.

The available UVU officers were eventually brought in.

The problem identified by the review is that these actions were not operating inside a complete enough, documented, coordinated security plan.

If you want one mental picture for this, imagine ten people each holding one piece of a map.

One person is dealing with the walkway.

One person is thinking about protests.

One person is handling arrival and departure.

One person knows about the EMP tip.

One person is thinking about ticketing.

One person has cameras.

One person knows the crowd estimate is wrong.

Everybody can be acting in good faith and still fail if nobody gets all ten pieces onto the same table.

That is why UVU’s recommendations after the fact matter.

The university now highlights written security assessments, formal Event Action Plans, documented elevated-vantage assessments, stronger coordination, clearer responsibilities and better advance planning for major events.

[SHOW #16]

Those recommendations do not prove liability.

They do show where the review believes the old process was insufficient.

And this helps explain why the “got you covered” text became so important.

In a mature security operation, four words in a text should not have to carry this much meaning.

There should be an event plan.

There should be a map.

There should be named assignments.

There should be a briefing.

There should be a walk-through.

There should be a record saying exactly who owns which zone.

Instead, after the assassination, the public is left asking whether “covered” meant one walkway or every elevated position surrounding the courtyard.

That is not a healthy place for any security system to end up.

But there is still one more branch of this story.

And unlike the UVU review, this one comes from the criminal court record.

It involves the man who helped facilitate Tyler Robinson’s surrender.

Mike Mitchell.

# 7. SIX MONTHS LATER, PROSECUTORS GO BACK TO MIKE MITCHELL

Mike Mitchell is described in the preliminary-hearing testimony as a close family friend of the Robinsons who helped facilitate Tyler Robinson’s surrender.

Tyler Robinson has pleaded not guilty. The criminal charges remain allegations unless proved at trial.

Mitchell was interviewed during the surrender period in September 2025.

Then, months later, investigators and prosecutors returned to him.

At the Day 3 preliminary hearing, Agent Brian Davis is asked whether a second interaction or statement was collected from Mitchell.

He says yes.

When?

March 31, 2026.

Who was there?

Members of the Utah County Attorney’s Office and SBI Agent Chris Terry.

Where did it happen?

At the St. George courthouse.

And then the testimony becomes very specific.

Mitchell was given a handwritten Rule 1102 statement form.

He went into a separate office by himself.

He stayed there for about an hour.

He wrote the statement.

Then he came back and gave it to the people waiting.

[SHOW #17]

You can watch the testimony itself beginning around the part of the hearing where the second Mitchell statement is introduced.

[SHOW #18]

Now, I want to be careful with the phrase “second statement.”

A second statement sounds dramatic.

But a second statement does not automatically mean a changed story.

It could supplement the first interview.

It could clarify something.

It could put an earlier oral account into the specific written format prosecutors wanted for a preliminary hearing.

It could add a detail investigators did not understand the significance of in September.

There are many normal reasons to return to a witness.

So the question is not:

“Why did he change his story?”

We do not know that he did.

The question is:

“What did the March 31 statement add, clarify or formalize?”

Then the state identifies the handwritten document as Exhibit 14.

The prosecutor says the defense and state have stipulated to redactions.

The state moves to admit it into evidence.

And then the prosecutor says they do not wish to publish it to the courtroom gallery or the cameras.

Judge Tony Graf reviews the exhibit and admits it.

But it is not published.

[SHOW #19]

Now that is interesting for a very specific reason.

We know the exhibit exists.

We know the judge saw it.

We know the parties saw it.

We know it was admitted for purposes of the preliminary hearing.

But the public watching the hearing did not get to read it.

That does not mean the contents were sinister.

Courts restrict publication for many legitimate reasons, including hearsay issues, privacy, prejudice and fair-trial concerns.

To understand why the document could be used at a preliminary hearing, you need Utah Rule of Evidence 1102.

The rule permits specified reliable hearsay in criminal preliminary examinations. One category includes a written, recorded or verbatim-transcribed statement made under oath or after notification that a false statement is punishable.

The rule also says prosecutors may assist a declarant in drafting a statement but may not draft it for the declarant or tamper with a witness.

[SHOW #20]

That is why the hearing spends time establishing how Mitchell wrote the statement.

Who was present.

Whether anyone told him what to say.

Whether he went into a room alone.

How long he was there.

Whether he received the warning about truthfulness.

That foundation matters because the prosecution wanted the statement admitted under the preliminary-hearing rules.

But the story does not end in July.

At the later final preliminary-hearing proceedings, prosecutors return to Exhibit 14.

This time they are not asking to publish the entire thing.

Ryan McBride describes what they want released as a “snip” of the relevant portion — three or four lines.

He says the full exhibit had not been published to the media or courtroom.

The defense objects.

Judge Graf denies publication of Exhibit 14.

[SHOW #21]

Later in the same proceeding, the state tells the court what at least part of Mitchell’s statement was being used to establish.

The prosecutor says Mitchell recounts a conversation in which Robinson allegedly stated that he had disposed of clothing, which the state connects to an obstruction-of-justice count.

That gives us some substantive context.

But it still does not give us the full statement.

So now the narrow question is stronger than it was before.

We are not simply asking why there was a second statement.

We know the statement eventually mattered enough to be admitted as an exhibit and cited by the state in support of a charged offense.

What we still cannot do is compare it line by line with Mitchell’s September account.

And that comparison is what would tell us whether the March statement was consistent, supplemental or materially different.

Until the underlying records become public, anybody declaring that Mitchell “changed his story” is going beyond what we can prove.

But anybody saying the second statement does not matter is also ignoring the fact that the prosecution used it as evidence.

The correct position is right in the middle.

It matters.

We do not yet know exactly how much.

# 8. NOW LOOK AT THE WHOLE RECORD WITHOUT CONNECTING WHAT WE CANNOT CONNECT

This is the part where I want to lay everything on one table.

Not because putting things next to each other proves a relationship.

Because chronology lets us see exactly what is documented and exactly where the public record stops.

September 8.

Dan Flood forwards a warning about student roof access close to Charlie’s setup.

The verbatim AAR says 3:12 p.m.

Chief Long asks whether Flood wants access to the roof.

Flood asks for controlled access or one of his own people.

Long replies:

“I got you covered.”

The AAR timeline appears to place the exchange at 4:11 p.m. and summarizes it as:

“We got you covered.”

The report later interprets the concern primarily as the Hall of Flags walkway.

The Kirk family’s notice of claim later frames the warning more broadly as rooftop security.

Those are different descriptions of the same underlying security conversation.

The original message export is the cleanest way to resolve the time and wording.

September 9.

At 2:23 p.m., a released UVU email says somebody wants the university to get a message to Charlie Kirk.

The name and phone number are redacted.

We do not know the content or disposition of the message.

We do not have the police-side record of that conversation in the material we are using tonight.

At 7:23 a.m. in the timeline, or approximately 7:30 in the narrative, Chief Long contacts SIAC about an anonymous EMP-device tip.

SIAC reportedly says the threat is not credible because the apparent tipster has provided similar unreliable information in the past.

We do not have the original tip or SIAC reply in front of us.

And now notice what I did not say.

I did not say the EMP tip was real intelligence.

I did not say the person trying to message Charlie was connected to the two GOP representatives.

I did not say those men were connected to Charlie’s death.

I did not say the “got you covered” wording was intentionally altered.

I did not say Mike Mitchell changed his story.

Those conclusions require evidence we do not have.

But the unanswered records are still worth pursuing.

The original Flood–Long message export.

The original EMP tip.

Chief Long’s SIAC email.

SIAC’s reply.

The complete chain surrounding UVU 25-330:0080.

The police contact record for the two September 9 visitors.

And Exhibit 14, if and when the court permits public access.

Those are not conspiracy demands.

They are ordinary documentary questions.

And here is the larger point.

People often think an investigation is about finding the most shocking explanation.

It is not.

A good investigation is often about eliminating explanations.

Maybe the original text export shows a simple transcription error.

Good. One mystery gone.

Maybe the EMP tip is obviously incoherent and SIAC’s rejection makes perfect sense.

Good. Another mystery gone.

Maybe the message for Charlie is a routine constituent-style request.

Good. Cross it off.

Maybe the two GOP representatives wanted to discuss ordinary event logistics.

Good. Close the branch.

Maybe Mitchell’s March statement matches his September interview almost perfectly and simply places it into Rule 1102 format.

Good. That tells us something.

The point is not to preserve mysteries.

The point is to resolve them.

And the fastest way to resolve them is to get closer to the primary record.

That is why I keep coming back to the same principle.

Do not give me somebody’s characterization if the original exists.

Do not give me a timeline summary if the message export exists.

Do not give me “non-credible tip” if the underlying tip can lawfully be released.

Do not give me “they talked to police” if there is a contact record showing what they discussed.

And do not tell me a second statement changed nothing or changed everything until we can compare the two statements.

That is the difference between being skeptical and being conspiratorial.

Skepticism says:

Show me the source.

Conspiracy thinking says:

I already know what the missing source must say.

Those are opposites.

# OUTRO

So where does this leave us?

With a report that documents real security failures while also saying it found no bad-faith conduct by UVU employees.

With one important security text presented two different ways inside that report.

With an anonymous EMP-related tip that was rejected by state intelligence but whose underlying communication is still not part of the public record we have reviewed.

With two unexplained September 9 contacts that appear in released UVU records but not in the AAR narrative.

And with a second Mike Mitchell statement that became evidence in the criminal case while remaining hidden from public view.

That is enough.

We do not need to invent the rest.

The story is already compelling when the standard is simple:

Show the original record, compare it to the story we were told, and follow the evidence only as far as it actually goes.
